## Why Remote Communication Deserves Focused Attention

      

The shift to remote and hybrid working has been one of the most significant workplace transformations in a generation. While many organisations have adapted their technology, far fewer have invested in developing the communication skills that remote teams actually need.

      

Without the benefit of hallway conversations, body language cues and shared physical spaces, remote teams must be far more intentional about how they communicate. Here are five tips drawn from our experience delivering communication skills training across the UK.

      

## 1. Default to Over-Communication

      

In a co-located office, information travels informally — overheard conversations, quick desk-side chats and shared whiteboards keep everyone roughly aligned. Remote teams lose all of this ambient awareness. The solution is to deliberately over-communicate.

      

Share context generously, summarise decisions in writing after video calls and keep shared channels updated. It may feel repetitive at first, but remote teams consistently tell us that too little communication causes far more problems than too much.

      

## 2. Choose the Right Channel for the Message

      

Not every message belongs in the same place. A useful framework is:

      

        - **Instant message:** Quick questions, casual check-ins, time-sensitive updates.
        - **Email:** Formal communications, external stakeholders, detailed information that needs a clear record.
        - **Video call:** Complex discussions, sensitive topics, brainstorming sessions and anything that benefits from real-time dialogue.
        - **Shared document:** Collaborative work, meeting notes, project plans that evolve over time.
      

      

Agreeing these norms as a team saves time and reduces frustration.

      

## 3. Build in Structured Social Time

      

Trust and rapport are the foundations of effective communication, and they are harder to build remotely. Schedule regular informal touchpoints — virtual coffee chats, team quizzes or simply a few minutes of non-work conversation at the start of meetings. These moments are not wasted time; they are an investment in the relationships that make collaboration possible.

      

## 4. Be Explicit About Expectations

      

When you cannot see whether a colleague is at their desk, assumptions fill the gap — and they are rarely accurate. Be clear about availability, response times and working hours. Encourage team members to set status messages and respect each other's boundaries. Clarity removes anxiety and builds mutual trust.

      

## 5. Invest in Active Listening Skills

      

Active listening is challenging enough in person; on a video call it requires even more discipline. Train your team to summarise what they have heard, ask clarifying questions and resist the temptation to multitask during meetings. When people feel genuinely heard, engagement and collaboration improve dramatically.
